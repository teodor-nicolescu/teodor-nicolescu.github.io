Research · Doctoral journey

From human behaviour to EEG-based deception research.

Studying concealed information through brain signals and data.

My doctoral research began with automated assessment of human behaviour among critical infrastructure personnel. Exploring physiological and psychological responses led me towards electroencephalography (EEG) and the computational study of concealed information.

Today, my research focuses on EEG-based deception recognition using artificial intelligence, with particular attention to signal processing, reproducible evaluation and the challenge of generalising across individuals.

Research perspective

A broad beginning.
A sharper research focus.

My initial doctoral topic explored automated systems for assessing human behaviour in critical infrastructure settings. Concealed behaviour was already an important concern in the earliest work, alongside psychological, physiological and contextual factors that can affect human responses.

The next reports progressively concentrated on EEG: first its potential for investigating deception-related cognitive processes, then its acquisition, preprocessing, mathematical modelling and advanced time–frequency analysis. The fifth report brought this methodological foundation into a more focused, empirically evaluated investigation of AI-based deception recognition, including the challenges of reliably distinguishing truth from deception across different people.

Current research directions

My research combines biomedical engineering, neurophysiological signal analysis and computational methods, with a particular focus on deception-related EEG.

EEG-Based Deception & Concealed Information

Selected research outputs

From foundations to published evidence.

Selected publications reflect the application of physiological signal analysis and artificial intelligence to deceptive behaviour and safety-related environments.

Published · Peer-reviewed2026 · Sensors (MDPI)

Deception Detection from Five-Channel Wearable EEG on LieWaves: A Reproducible Baseline for Subject-Dependent and Subject-Independent Evaluation

Tests five-channel EEG data using a reproducible framework, contrasting subject-dependent with subject-independent evaluation. The primary cross-subject analysis reports 66.70% session-level accuracy (AUC 57.80%); the study emphasises the limits of generalisation and does not establish a deployment-ready lie detector.

DOI: 10.3390/s26031027
